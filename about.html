The style has a name.
His name.

This tool is an unaffiliated homage to the Lessig Method — the rapid-fire, one-idea-per-slide presentation style pioneered by Lawrence Lessig. We named it "Lessig-ish" on purpose: the credit is his, the software is ours.

Who is Lawrence Lessig?

Lessig is a Harvard Law professor, a founding voice of internet law, co-founder of Creative Commons, and author of Free Culture. Long before he was a household name in tech policy, he was the person who figured out how to make a room full of people staring at their laptops look up.

What is the Lessig Method?

He was speaking at tech conferences where the audience sat glued to their screens, and he needed a way to pull their eyes to the front. His answer: a great many slides, each holding just one word, one phrase, or one image — fired in rapid succession, timed precisely to his spoken words, rarely lingering more than a few seconds.

The slides aren't the presentation; they're signposts. Each one lands a single concept the way we actually learn — one at a time. It shifts the whole weight of the talk from the deck onto the speaker. Which is exactly why it's so hard to do by hand, and exactly what this tool automates: the words find their timing against a real voice, and the film assembles itself.

The talks that made it famous

  • "Free Culture" — OSCON 2002 keynote — the origin: 243 slides synced to the audio, on intellectual property and creativity. The one everyone traces the method back to.
  • "Laws that choke creativity" — TED — Lessig delivering the method to a wide audience.
  • Dick Hardt, "Identity 2.0" — OSCON 2005 — the most-cited adopter; often held up as one of the best executions of the method by someone other than Lessig.
  • Presentation Zen — "The Lessig Method" — Garr Reynolds' well-known breakdown of why it works.
  • Wes Fryer — Lessig Method presentation — an early, generous write-up from an educator who adopted it.
